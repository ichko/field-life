What this is

The rule from the flat page, unchanged, on a cube of cells instead of a sheet of them. Each colour is a scalar field over an N×N×N grid. Every step convolves each colour's field with a spherical kernel, mixes the colours through the interaction matrix into one number per cell — how much that colour wants to be there — and then hands every cell's contents to its 27 neighbours in proportion to exp(beta·A). Nothing is created and nothing is destroyed: the mass readout under the field is measured on the GPU each time it is shown, and it does not move off its opening value.

How big the grid can be

96³ holds sixty frames on ordinary hardware and is the default. It goes to 256³ if the card has the memory, and past about 128³ the thing to reach for is the rate control rather than a smaller grid: at a step every second or fourth frame a very large field still turns over at a watchable speed while the picture stays at the frame rate. The grid menu prints what each size costs before you pick it, and sizes the card cannot hold are not offered.

The reason it goes that far is that the expensive half of the step does not run at the grid's own pitch. A neighbourhood integral out to a dozen cells does not change measurably if it is taken on a grid with half the spacing, and in three dimensions halving the pitch is eight times less work, not four. So the field is downsampled once, the two Gaussians and the affinity are computed there, and only the exponent and the transport — the parts that have to be crisp — run at full pitch. The kernels are separable, which matters far more here than it does in two dimensions: a spherical stencil of half-width 8 is 4913 taps, three 1-D passes are 51.

Those are for up to four colours. Past four it takes a second layer of everything, so five to eight colours cost twice the memory and twice the step — the trade is stated in the menu. A step is about 60 texture reads per cell all told, six full-screen draws plus one pass that has to go slice by slice. That last one is the only place a 3D texture is written, because a 3D texture takes one draw call per slice where a 2D atlas of the same slices takes one for all of them. Everything the step reads back by index — the blurs, the affinity, the exponent, the shares — lives in an atlas for exactly that reason. The slice pass writes four at a time, and since neighbouring slices share 18 of their 27 taps it sums nine columns once and slides a window of three down them: half the reads for four cells at once.

How it is shaded

March and integrate, not trace. There is no surface to intersect — the field is a density everywhere — so the only honest picture is the emission-absorption integral along each ray. The question worth answering is which end to start from, and it is the near end, for the reason you would expect: marching front to back carries a running transmittance that says how much of what is still ahead can possibly reach the eye, so the moment it falls under a few thousandths the rest of the ray is worth nothing and the loop stops. Back to front cannot do that — it has to visit everything before it knows what was hidden.

Two more things come out of the same idea, that effort should follow how much a sample can still change the pixel: the step grows as transmittance falls, so a sample that will be attenuated to a tenth gets a tenth of the care; and shadow rays are rationed — each lit sample gets a short march towards the light, but only if it can contribute more than a thousandth of the pixel, and below that the last shadow value stands, which is fine because shadow varies slowly along a ray.

Empty space is handled separately, by a second volume of one cell per 8³ block holding that block's peak density and its mean. The peak lets a ray in empty space jump straight to the far wall of its block instead of stepping through it, which is most of the cube most of the time. The mean is what the shadow march reads: a 12³ texture that never leaves cache, so self-shadowing — the thing that actually makes the cloud read as solid — costs less than one more sample of the real field would.

The light is a key held at a fixed angle from the view rather than from the world, since a world-fixed light spends half of every orbit directly behind the camera and a volume lit from behind the camera has no shading at all. On top of it: a sky ambient dimmed by how buried the sample is, a Henyey-Greenstein lobe so a cloud lit from behind glows at its rim, and one density gradient taken once per ray at the depth the cloud first turned solid, which buys the whole image a sense of surface for six taps instead of six times the march. That last one is the specular, and specular at zero removes it completely — highlight, rim and all — leaving key and ambient and nothing else.

Seeing into it

A cloud lit properly shows you its skin and hides everything behind it, which is exactly wrong for watching a simulation. Four views, and the V key cycles them:

viewwhat it shows
solidthe full shading. What it looks like, not what it is doing
flatthe same depth order with the lighting taken away: colour and shape, no relief
x-raynothing occludes anything, every sample adds. The inside is as visible as the skin
peakthe densest sample along each ray wins. One structure per pixel, cleanly

The last two cannot stop early — that is what seeing through means — so they lean on the empty-space skip instead and cost rather more. The gain slider is their exposure.

The cut is the other half of it: pick an axis and the picture is clipped to a slab of the cube, with from and to as its two ends. Pull one in and you have a cross-section through the middle of the thing; bring the two together and you have a single plane of the cube, which is the flat page's picture again, one slice at a time. It is not a shader trick painted over the volume: the marched box really is that slab, so a cut is also the cheapest the renderer ever gets. The wireframe follows the cut, with the whole domain left faint behind it.

Where did that colour go

It did not go anywhere — the readout says so. What usually happens is that a colour concentrates: its mean over the cube is fixed for the life of the run, so if the peak climbs, the field it came from must have emptied out. That is why the readout carries both. mass is the mean per colour and never moves; peak is the densest 8³ block per colour, and when it walks up into the tens, that colour has pulled itself into knots small enough to vanish between the ray's samples, and usually knots buried inside something else, which the front-to-back march quite correctly refuses to show you.

Three things to reach for, in order. The show chips turn colours off one at a time — double-click one to solo it — which settles the question in a second. The threshold is the density below which the picture treats the field as haze and cuts it; a colour spread thin lives entirely under it, so lowering it brings the thin colour back. And if the peak really is climbing, the world is over-concentrating: more repel, or less force or beta, is the fix, and it is a change to the rule rather than to the picture.

Handling it

Drag to orbit, wheel to zoom, shift-drag to paint and alt-drag to erase. Painting in a volume needs a depth, and the one it uses is the depth you are looking at: a single ray is marched at the cursor, the same way the picture is, and the brush lands where transmittance first drops through a half. Space holds it, R reseeds, N draws a new world, V cycles the view, C cuts through, H hides the panel, F is fullscreen.

The opening field has five shapes — a ball, a cloud filling the cube, a hollow shell, a handful of spots, and dust — and how much mass there is does not depend on which. The density asked for is a mean over the whole cube and is divided out after the shape is drawn, so a small ball is a dense one and a large one is thin. That is the dial to reach for when the opening blob is too solid to see into: make it bigger, or give it less mass.

The matrix under MATRIX is the world. Row c, column d is how hard colour c is pulled towards what its kernel sees of colour d; drag a cell up or down to change it, live. It need not be symmetric, and the asymmetry is where the chasing comes from.

This is a staging page — a test of whether the rule and the rendering both hold up in three dimensions, not a finished thing.
