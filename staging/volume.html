What this is

The rule from the flat page, unchanged, on a cube of cells instead of a sheet of them. Each colour is a scalar field over an N×N×N grid. Every step convolves each colour's field with a spherical kernel, mixes the colours through the interaction matrix into one number per cell — how much that colour wants to be there — and then hands every cell's contents to its 27 neighbours in proportion to exp(beta·A). Nothing is created and nothing is destroyed: the mass readout is measured on the GPU each time it is shown, and it does not move off its opening value.

How big the grid can be

Short answer: 96³ holds sixty frames on ordinary hardware, 128³ on a discrete card, and 160³ if you have something serious. The counter under the field says what your machine is actually doing rather than what this paragraph guesses.

The reason it goes that far is that the expensive half of the step does not run at the grid's own pitch. A neighbourhood integral out to a dozen cells does not change measurably if it is taken on a grid with half the spacing, and in three dimensions halving the pitch is eight times less work, not four. So the field is downsampled once, the two Gaussians and the affinity are computed there, and only the exponent and the transport — the parts that have to be crisp — run at full pitch. The kernels are separable, which matters far more here than it does in two dimensions: a spherical stencil of half-width 8 is 4913 taps, three 1-D passes are 51.

A step costs about 60 texture reads per cell all told, and it is six full-screen draws plus one pass that has to go slice by slice. That last one is the only place the 3D texture is written, because a 3D texture takes one draw call per slice where a 2D atlas of the same slices takes one for all of them. Everything the step reads back by index — the blurs, the affinity, the exponent, the shares — lives in an atlas for exactly that reason. The slice pass writes four at a time, and since neighbouring slices share 18 of their 27 taps it sums nine columns once and slides a window of three down them: half the reads for four cells at once.

How it is shaded

March and integrate, not trace. There is no surface to intersect — the field is a density everywhere — so the only honest picture is the emission-absorption integral along each ray. The question worth answering is which end to start from, and it is the near end, for the reason you would expect: marching front to back carries a running transmittance that says how much of what is still ahead can possibly reach the eye, so the moment it falls under a few thousandths the rest of the ray is worth nothing and the loop stops. Back to front cannot do that — it has to visit everything before it knows what was hidden.

Two more things come out of the same idea, that effort should follow how much a sample can still change the pixel:

— the step grows as transmittance falls. A sample that will be attenuated to a tenth gets roughly a tenth of the care. Deep cloud is where the sample count would otherwise go.

— shadow rays are rationed. Each lit sample is shadowed by a short march towards the light, but only if it can contribute more than a thousandth of the pixel; below that the last shadow value stands, which is fine because shadow varies slowly along a ray.

Empty space is handled separately, by a second volume of one cell per 8³ block holding that block's peak density and its mean. The peak lets a ray in empty space jump straight to the far wall of its block instead of stepping through it, which is most of the cube most of the time. The mean is what the shadow march reads: it is a 12³ texture that never leaves cache, so self-shadowing — the thing that actually makes the cloud read as solid — costs less than one more sample of the real field would.

The rest is one key light with a Henyey-Greenstein lobe so a cloud lit from behind glows at its rim, a sky ambient dimmed by how buried the sample is, and a single density gradient taken once per ray at the depth the cloud first turned solid, which buys the whole image a sense of surface for six taps instead of six times the march. The threshold slider subtracts a constant from the density before any of that: haze is what makes a volume look like fog rather than like a thing, and cutting it also hands more of the cube back to the empty-space skip.

The picture is marched at a fraction of the canvas when it has to be — the counter says which — and the frame time drives that fraction, so the field keeps its resolution and the picture of it goes soft instead. Hold still with the simulation paused and the frames average together, which is what turns a preview into a rendering.

Handling it

Drag to orbit, wheel to zoom, shift-drag to paint and alt-drag to erase. Painting in a volume needs a depth, and the one it uses is the depth you are looking at: a single ray is marched at the cursor, the same way the picture is, and the brush lands where transmittance first drops through a half. Space holds it, R reseeds, N draws a new world, H hides the panel, F is fullscreen.

The matrix under MATRIX is the world. Row c, column d is how hard colour c is pulled towards what its kernel sees of colour d; drag a cell up or down to change it, live. It need not be symmetric, and the asymmetry is where the chasing comes from.

This is a staging page — a test of whether the rule and the rendering both hold up in three dimensions, not a finished thing.
