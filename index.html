How it works

Every color is a field: one number in every cell of the grid saying how much of that color is sitting there. Not a swarm of dots — a smear of stuff. Six colors on a 512 grid means six numbers per cell, about a million and a half numbers in total, all updated on the GPU every frame.

One step has two halves. First work out where each color would like to be. Then move the mass there — move, never create, never destroy.

The kernel is particle life’s own force law, unchanged: strong repulsion at touching distance, easing to nothing at 30% of the radius, then a triangle of attraction that peaks midway out and fades to zero at the edge. Sliding it over a color field answers “which way, and how hard, does this cell get pulled by that color?” Every pair of colors has its own radius, set in the second matrix.

f(x) = x/β − 1  if x < β,   else 1 − |2x−1−β| / (1−β) particle life’s force against distance x (as a fraction of the radius), with β = 0.3
Ac = force · ∑d M[c][d] · (f ∗ ρd) − repel · crowding where color c wants to be: the matrix says how it feels about every color, minus a dislike of crowds
share(y → x) = eβA(x) ⁄ Z(y) the fraction of cell y’s mass that goes to neighbour x. Z is the total over y’s nine cells, so the nine shares add to exactly 1

That last line is the whole reason nothing ever explodes or dies out. Each cell gives away shares of its own mass, and the shares sum to one — so every unit that leaves somewhere arrives somewhere. Add it all up and the total is the same number forever, which is what the bars on the right are showing you.

Change the matrix and you change what chases what. Because it need not be symmetric, red can be drawn to green while green flees red — and that one asymmetry is where most of the chasing, orbiting and swarming comes from.

Built on

  1. MaCE — the transport rule used here: each cell hands its mass to its nine neighbours in proportion to eβA, so mass is conserved by construction. Papadopoulos, V. & Guichard, E. (2025). MaCE: General Mass Conserving Dynamics for Cellular Automata. arXiv:2507.12306
  2. Particle Life — the interaction matrix and short-range repulsion, in its original swarm-of-dots form. Mohr, T., particle-life.com, after Ventrella, J., Clusters, ventrella.com/Clusters
  3. Lenia — the continuous cellular automaton lineage: kernels convolved with a field, fed through a growth function. Chan, B. W.-C. (2019). Lenia — Biology of Artificial Life. Complex Systems 28(3), 251–286. arXiv:1812.05433
  4. Flow-Lenia — the nearest relative: per-channel affinity, an anti-crowding term and mass conservation on a grid, via reintegration tracking rather than a softmax. Plantec, E., Hamon, G., Etcheverry, M., Chan, B. W.-C., Oudeyer, P.-Y. & Moulin-Frier, C. (2023). arXiv:2212.07906
  5. Non-reciprocal Cahn–Hilliard — the same asymmetric-matrix idea as continuum physics, where it produces travelling waves and “chasing” phases. Saha, S., Agudo-Canalejo, J. & Golestanian, R. (2020). Phys. Rev. X 10, 041009. arXiv:2005.07101
